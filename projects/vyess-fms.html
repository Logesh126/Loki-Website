The Big Idea

A simple service experience often requires a complex system behind it.

Booking a home service might take a few clicks. But behind that experience are quotations, scheduling, vendor coordination, payments, service verification, and customer support.

Vyess needed a digital ecosystem that could bring all these moving parts together without making the experience complicated for the people using it.

My goal was to design a platform where complexity stayed within the system, not in the user's way.

Understanding the Product

Vyess FMS is a home and facility management platform designed to connect customers with service providers while giving internal teams the tools to manage the complete service lifecycle.

The platform brings together three interconnected experiences:

Customer Experience

Discover services, request quotations, book appointments, track orders, and manage service-related activities.

Vendor Experience

Receive service requests, manage assignments, update job progress, and track earnings.

Admin Experience

Manage services, customers, vendors, quotations, assignments, payments, offers, and customer support.

The challenge was not simply to design three interfaces. It was to make them work as one connected product.

Three Challenges That Shaped the Product

01 — Customer Confidence

Customers needed a predictable booking experience, clear pricing, reliable scheduling, and visibility into what happens after placing an order.

02 — Vendor Coordination

Vendors needed a structured way to receive requests, accept or reject assignments, manage service progress, and complete jobs.

03 — Operational Control

The internal team needed visibility across bookings, quotations, payments, vendor assignments, and complaints without depending entirely on manual coordination.

The design challenge was to balance all three without compromising the experience of any user.

My Thinking

Before designing screens, I designed the system.

I started by understanding the business model and mapping how a service moves through the platform. I examined the relationships between customers, administrators, and vendors, identifying where decisions, handovers, and potential friction occurred.

This helped me establish the underlying service lifecycle before translating it into individual interfaces.

Discover→Request→Quote→Book→Assign→Service→Verify→Pay→Complete

Not every service followed the same path. That became one of the most important design considerations.

Four Product Decisions That Shaped the Experience

01 — One Booking Experience. Two Service Models.

Not every home service can be priced in advance. Some services have fixed prices, while others require an inspection or quotation before booking.

Instead of forcing both into the same transaction logic, I designed a shared shopping and booking experience that adapts to the service type.

Fixed-price services

Discover→Add to Cart→Select Slot→Checkout→Payment→Booking Confirmed

Quotation-based services

Request Quotation→Admin/Vendor Pricing→Customer Reviews Quote→Accept→Book→Service→Payment

I also accounted for mixed carts. If a cart contains quotation-based services, the quotation workflow takes priority rather than prematurely treating the entire cart as a fixed-price order.

Design principle: Consistency in the experience, flexibility in the business logic.

02 — Customer Confirmation Before Payment.

A service should not be considered complete simply because a vendor marks it as finished. I designed a customer-first completion workflow to introduce an additional verification step.

Vendor Completes Service→Customer Inspects→Customer Confirms→Easebuzz Payment→Payment Confirmation→Job Closure & Earnings

If the customer is dissatisfied, they can contact support or raise a complaint through the platform before proceeding. This established a clearer responsibility boundary between service completion, customer approval, and payment.

Design principle: Build trust into the workflow, not just the interface.

03 — Structured Vendor Assignment & Reassignment.

Vendor coordination needed to be predictable and manageable. I designed a structured assignment workflow with a five-minute response window.

Admin Assigns Vendor→Vendor Receives Request→Accept / Reject within 5 min

If the vendor rejects the request, the rejection reason and supporting details are recorded. If the vendor does not respond within five minutes, the assignment automatically expires and the order returns to the assignment queue. The admin can review the previous rejection or expiry details before assigning another vendor.

Design principle: Make operational exceptions visible and actionable.

04 — One Connected Operational System.

Rather than treating the admin and vendor dashboards as collections of unrelated pages, I approached them as interconnected operational environments.

The admin dashboard was structured around service management, orders, quotations, vendor assignments, payments, customer management, complaints, coupons, offers, and records. The vendor dashboard focused on incoming requests, active services, service history, and earnings.

I also introduced a WordPress-inspired service management structure, allowing administrators to manage categories, subcategories, and detailed service information through a hierarchical interface. This created a modular foundation that could accommodate additional services and operational features.

Design principle: Design systems that can grow with the business.

Designing the Complete Experience

Customer Experience

The customer-facing experience was designed around discoverability, confidence, and convenience.

  • Service discovery and category navigation
  • Detailed service information
  • Fixed-price and quotation-based booking
  • Cart and multi-service checkout
  • Date, time, and address selection
  • OTP-based authentication
  • Order tracking and notifications
  • Quotations and pricing decisions
  • Coupons and offers
  • Customer profile and document centre
  • Reviews, feedback, and complaints
  • Gold Membership experience

A key authentication decision was to allow customers to explore and add services to the cart before logging in. Mobile OTP authentication was introduced when they proceeded to checkout, with email collected for invoicing and billing.

Admin Experience

The admin experience was designed as the central operational control system.

  • Dashboard overview
  • Order and quotation management
  • Manual order creation
  • Vendor assignment and reassignment
  • Service and category management
  • Payment control
  • Vendor and customer management
  • Coupon and offer management
  • Complaint management
  • Notifications and historical records

Vendor Experience

The vendor experience prioritised clear actions and minimal friction.

  • Incoming service requests
  • Accept and reject workflows
  • Rejection reason capture
  • Assignment expiry information
  • Active service management
  • Service progress updates
  • Earnings and service history

An additional privacy decision restricted vendor access to customer details until two hours before the scheduled service. This helped protect customer information while preserving the information needed for service delivery.

Designing for Real Operational Scenarios

A product is not defined only by its ideal user journey. It must also handle situations where things do not go as planned.

Vendor Rejection: Captures structured rejection reasons and additional notes before returning an order for reassignment.

Assignment Expiry: Automatically returns unanswered requests to the assignment queue after the five-minute response window.

Customer Dissatisfaction: Provides a support and complaint pathway instead of allowing an immediate job closure.

Quotation Decisions: Allows customers to review, accept, or reject proposed service pricing.

Complaint Resolution: Keeps the support workflow aligned with the business process, where support contacts customers by phone and updates the complaint status.

These scenarios helped make the product more practical beyond the happy path.

Design Language

Consistency across three experiences.

A reusable design system was developed to maintain visual and interaction consistency throughout the platform.

  • Typography and hierarchy
  • Colour palette
  • Spacing and layout rules
  • Buttons and form controls
  • Service cards
  • Navigation patterns
  • Tables and data views
  • Status indicators
  • Modals and confirmation dialogs
  • Responsive components

The objective was to establish a consistent visual language while allowing each user role to have an interface suited to its responsibilities.

What Changed

The design established a connected product foundation for managing the complete home service lifecycle.

  • Structured the experience across three distinct user roles.
  • Unified fixed-price and quotation-based service journeys.
  • Introduced clearer customer verification and payment stages.
  • Established structured vendor assignment and reassignment workflows.
  • Organised service and operational management into modular dashboard experiences.
  • Created reusable design patterns for consistency and future expansion.

The focus was not simply on creating more interfaces, but on making the relationships between them easier to understand and manage.

What I Took Away

This project changed how I approach product design.

I realised that designing a complex product is not about solving individual screen-level problems. It is about understanding how different people, decisions, and workflows connect.

Vyess challenged me to think beyond the customer interface and consider the operational system behind every interaction.

It strengthened my ability to balance user expectations, business requirements, operational constraints, and long-term scalability.

Most importantly, it taught me to design for the entire experience—not just the moment a user interacts with a screen.
